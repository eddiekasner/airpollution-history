## Slides 1-2: Title and overview (1:00)

**Slide 1. Title**

- Welcome; this video replaces part of in-class lecture so we can use class time for discussion
- Goal: see how air pollution disasters shaped what we know about exposure and the laws we have today

**Slide 2. Overview**

- Start with vocabulary: sources, routes, pathways
- Then six events across almost a century, from a Belgian valley to recent wildfire smoke
- Watch for the pattern: source + weather + people = disaster, then science and policy respond
- Close with key studies and the policies that came out of these events

## Slides 3-5: Exposure basics (3:00)

**Slide 3. Exposure route**

- Pathway = source + medium + receptor; break any link and there is no exposure
- Route = how a contaminant gets into the body: skin contact, ingestion, inhalation
- For air pollution, inhalation is the main route, but particles that settle can also be swallowed or touched
- Image comes from a farmworker pesticide paper; same three routes apply to any chemical

**Slide 4. Air pollution sources**

- Walk the picture left to right: volcanoes and fires, farms, factories, vehicles, ships, planes
- Point out the arrows: some pollutants come straight out of a source, others form up in the air
- Ask students: which of these sources are near where you live or commute?

**Slide 5. Exposure pathway**

- Sources can be natural or human; most of today's events are human-driven
- Primary pollutants are emitted directly (CO, NOx, SO2, PM, NH3, VOCs, lead)
- Secondary pollutants form in the atmosphere; ozone is the classic example and returns in LA
- Quick win to mention: lead was taken out of gasoline, and blood lead levels dropped
- Receptors matter: each pollutant has different health effects, and some people are more vulnerable
- Do not memorize every chemical; focus on primary vs. secondary

## Slides 6-8: Meuse Valley and temperature inversion (3:00)

**Slide 6. Meuse Valley map**

- December 1930, an industrial valley in eastern Belgium between Liège and Huy
- Show how narrow the valley is and how many factories line the river
- Shape of the land is part of the story: steep walls hold air in place

**Slide 7. Meuse Valley details**

- Sources: SO2, sulfuric acid, possibly fluoride from zinc, glass, and steel plants
- Several days of cold, still air trapped the emissions in the valley
- Receptors: asthma-like symptoms; about 60 excess deaths, mostly older adults and people with heart or lung disease
- At the time, pollution was seen as the price of prosperity
- Doctors and investigators interviewed patients right after; this is the first scientific proof that air pollution can kill

**Slide 8. Temperature inversion**

- Normally air is warmer near the ground and rises, carrying pollution up and away
- In an inversion, a warm layer sits on top like a lid; cold air and pollution are stuck below
- Valleys and basins make inversions worse
- Local tie-in: Puget Sound gets winter inversions too, which is why burn bans happen

## Slides 9-10: Donora (2:00)

**Slide 9. Donora photo**

- Late October 1948, a mill town of about 14,000 on the Monongahela River near Pittsburgh
- Point to the smokestacks and how close homes sit to the mills
- Same setup as Meuse: river valley, heavy industry, still weather

**Slide 10. Donora details**

- Sources: zinc smelter and steel mill emissions (CO, SO2, sulfuric acid) held down by an inversion for 5 days
- Nearly half the town got sick; about 20 people died during the event
- The zinc works shut down on October 31, and rain cleared the air that same day
- First US air pollution disaster to get national attention
- Helped lead to the first federal air law, the Air Pollution Control Act of 1955, which funded research
- Takeaway: disasters create the political will that data alone often cannot

## Slides 11-12: Great Smog of London (2:30)

**Slide 11. London photo**

- December 1952; a tugboat on the Thames near Tower Bridge, barely visible
- Smog so thick buses stopped, and people could not see their own feet

**Slide 12. London details**

- Source: a cold snap, so millions of homes burned extra cheap coal (SO2 and soot)
- An inversion parked a stagnant air mass over the city for 5 days
- About 100,000 people sick; at least 4,000 died that week, and later estimates reach 12,000
- Most deaths were older adults and people with heart or lung disease, mainly from bronchitis and pneumonia
- Pop culture hook: *The Crown* (season 1) dramatized this event
- Impact: UK Clean Air Act of 1956, which created smoke-free zones and moved homes off coal
- Why epidemiologists care: the first detailed analysis that tied measured pollutant levels to illness and death day by day
- Scale matters: Meuse and Donora were towns; London was a world capital

## Slides 13-15: Los Angeles and the Clean Air Act (3:00)

**Slide 13. 1950s gas masks**

- A civic club banquet around 1954, everyone in gas masks
- Half joke, half protest: LA residents were fed up with burning eyes and sore throats

**Slide 14. LA skyline in smog**

- A different kind of smog: brown and hazy on sunny days, not a black winter fog
- Ask students: what is different about the weather here compared with London?

**Slide 15. LA details**

- Source: cars; over a million on LA roads by 1940
- Secondary pollution: VOCs + NOx + sunlight make ozone (photochemical smog)
- Geography and weather trap it: a basin ringed by mountains, plus inversions from the cold ocean current
- Receptors: breathing and heart problems; by 1961 respiratory deaths tracked with ozone across the region
- Impact: the US Clean Air Act of 1970 set national limits for factories and vehicles, plus catalytic converters and unleaded gas
- Ozone today is far below 1970s peaks despite many more cars: a real public health win
- But LA was still ranked worst in the US for ozone in the 2026 State of the Air report: progress is not the finish line

## Slide 16: US emissions after the Clean Air Act (0:45)

- National emissions of the main pollutants, 1990-2024, from EPA's Our Nation's Air 2025; CO uses the right axis because it is so much larger
- SO2 fell the most, over 90%, thanks to power plant scrubbers and the 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments acid rain program
- NOx, CO, and VOCs also dropped sharply: cleaner cars, catalytic converters, power plant controls
- Ammonia (NH3) stayed flat or rose: mostly from agriculture, and largely unregulated
- Big picture: EPA reports combined emissions of the common pollutants fell 79% from 1970 to 2024 while the economy, population, and driving grew
- Bridge to Beijing: regulation works, and other countries faced the same choice later

## Slides 17-18: Beijing "Airpocalypse" (2:00)

**Slide 17. Beijing split screen**

- Same skyline on a clear day and a smog day in 2014
- Shows how quickly a city can disappear when an inversion sets in

**Slide 18. Beijing details**

- Sources: coal for winter heating, heavy industry, traffic; like LA, a bowl with inversions
- January 2013: PM2.5 went off the top of the Air Quality Index, more than 20 times the WHO guideline
- The US Embassy posted hourly readings online, and public pressure grew
- Impact: China's 2013 Air Pollution Action Plan cut coal use, closed old plants, and pushed cleaner fuels
- Result: Beijing's annual PM2.5 fell from about 100 µg/m³ in 2013 to 27 µg/m³ in 2025
- Same lesson as London and LA: crisis, then policy, then measurable improvement; still above WHO guidelines

## Slides 19-20: Recent wildfire smoke events (2:00)

**Slide 19. West Coast wildfire smoke**

- NASA satellite view of smoke spreading across the western US (this image is from 2017)
- Point out how the plumes cover multiple states, far beyond the red fire spots
- Shift in the story: the big events now are less about factories and more about fire

**Slide 20. Recent wildfire smoke events**

- Sources: hotter, drier conditions and strong winds drive bigger fires; the smoke is mostly PM2.5
- Receptors: whole regions, rural and urban, often hundreds of miles from the fire
- September 2020, our backyard: Portland had the world's worst major-city air; Seattle's daily AQI peaked near 200 and Portland's at 357
- June 2023: smoke from Canadian fires, mostly in Quebec, pushed New York City's AQI near the top of the scale (500); about 62 million people were exposed
- January 2025, Los Angeles: wind-driven urban fires destroyed over 16,000 structures; burning homes and cars put heavy metals in the smoke and ash
- Contrast with earlier events: you cannot regulate a smokestack here, so protection relies on AQI alerts, indoor air filtration, and limiting time outdoors
- Ask students: has wildfire smoke changed your plans in the last few summers?

## Slide 21: Air pollution studies (2:30)

- Disasters showed short-term harm; long-term studies showed everyday pollution matters too
- Harvard Six Cities (1993): people in dirtier cities died sooner, even at levels considered normal
- MESA Air, led by UW: long-term exposure linked to faster buildup of calcium in heart arteries
- ACT-AP, here at UW: each 1 µg/m³ increase in long-term PM2.5 linked to about 16% higher dementia risk (2021)
- State of Global Air 2024: air pollution was linked to 8.1 million deaths worldwide in 2021, second only to high blood pressure as a risk factor
- Burke et al. 2023: wildfire smoke erased about a quarter of the PM2.5 progress made since 2000 in 35 states; connects back to the last two slides
- Invite students to check the PSCAA sensor map, Ecology's map, or the AirNow Fire and Smoke Map this week
- Health effects to remember: early death, respiratory disease and lung cancer, heart disease and stroke, low birthweight, dementia

## Slide 22: Government and policy, plus closing (2:30)

- Clean Air Acts: UK 1956 after London, US 1970 after decades of LA smog
- NAAQS: EPA sets limits for six "criteria" pollutants (CO, lead, NO2, ozone, PM, SO2)
- Standards change as science grows: in 2024 the annual PM2.5 limit dropped from 12 to 9 µg/m³, and a federal court upheld it in 2026
- WHO's 2021 guideline is even stricter at 5 µg/m³; health-based guidelines and legal standards are not the same thing
- Modeling and monitoring: dispersion models predict where pollution goes; receptor models trace it back to sources; PSCAA and Ecology run local monitors
- Washington's wildfire smoke rule (2024, updated 2025) requires employers to protect outdoor workers as PM2.5 rises: policy catching up to the wildfire era
- Climate Commitment Act: Ecology funds pollution cuts and more monitoring in 16 overburdened communities, an environmental justice approach to air quality

**Closing**

- Recap the pattern: source + weather + people, then science, then policy
- Each event moved us forward, but clean air takes ongoing work
- Bring one question to class: who is most exposed today, and why?
